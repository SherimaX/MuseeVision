Albion

England in the Salon’s decades

Through the Rotunda’s south door, a court of iron and glass on ten columns of polished British stone, after the Oxford University Museum that Ruskin championed. While Paris painted the passing moment in broken colour, London painted legend and poetry in hard detail, and turned art into glass, cloth and books. A museum court, not a church: one storey under three glass roofs.

London keeps the weather

The glass shows the sky over London right now: rain running down it, fog in the vaults, a bright hour. The Rotunda keeps the hour, the Chinese garden the season, Albion the weather.

The sun at the end

The nave runs due south and ends in iron and clear glass, like a Victorian train shed. On a bright London day the Tristram and Isolde band lays its colours up the floor.

A stone from every corner

Ten columns, each a different polished British stone, as at the Oxford University Museum; each capital carved with plants from the paintings or from Morris.

A book you can open

The Kelmscott Chaucer, 1896, on a lectern in Morris’s bay: turn its pages, with woodcuts after Burne-Jones in Morris’s own type.
