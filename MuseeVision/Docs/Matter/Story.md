# The material story

*Classical bones, modern light, future glass.* This is the museum's material story, as D-018 asks: which real
material stands behind every surface, why it belongs in this museum, and how it changes finish from era to era. The
data is in `Data/Matter/Palette.json`, one entry per palette key. Every room's finish schedule
(`Data/Stages/<Room>/Finishes.json`) names a palette key and a finish from this page, and nothing else.

This page chooses materials; it does not give values. Each chosen material's numbers come from its Matter record
(`Data/Matter/Substances/<key>.json`), measured and sourced. Where a record does not exist yet, the palette says so.

## 1. The idea in one paragraph

The museum is built of four families, and all four are the same families a Roman or Parisian builder would have
recognised: **one stone, one white, one bronze, one oak**. The stone is **Roman travertine from Tivoli**, the stone
of the Colosseum and of Bernini's colonnade, and also of the Getty and of Gae Aulenti's Orsay, so it belongs to the
classical and the modern at once. At the anchor, the Rotunda, the travertine gives way to a finer, paler, denser
stone, **Crema Marfil from Monte Coto**. It returns at the very end of the visit, polished as far as stone can be
polished, as the floor of Élan. The white is **lime**: lime plaster in the classical vaults, then limewash in the
garden, then, in the future, a **pearl-coated steel** that is the white made in a factory. The metal is **one tin
bronze, CuSn10**, the casting bronze of antiquity, in every fitting and in the strip at every doorway. The wood is
**French sessile oak**, quartersawn, for everything the hand and foot touch. The eras change the **finish**:
- Classical is honed, patinated, oiled and waxed.
- Modern is polished, brushed, and fumed and brushed.
- Future is mirror-polished and gilt, and the oak leaves, because in Élan nothing is handled: the car carries you.

A second thread runs under all of this, and it is physical. **Iron** colours this museum wherever it has colour:
- the honey of the travertine;
- the grey-brown seams in the Rotunda's marble;
- the red bole under every gilding;
- the Pompeian red of the casts' niches;
- the red band on the sun clock;
- Albion's red sandstone and its iron painted in the colour of its own oxide;
- Chenghuai's iron-red columns, and its grey brick, which is the same iron fired without air;
- the green edge of Albion's Victorian glass.

At the end of the visit the iron is taken out. Élan's glass is **low-iron** glass, water-white: the future is the
one place in the museum without iron. The accents all tie back to the core families along this thread. The visitor
never needs to know it for the rooms to feel like one building. It is why they do.

## 2. The four families, and the real material behind each

### 2.1 Stone: Travertino Romano, and the Rotunda's Crema Marfil

**Travertine: Travertino Romano Classico, from the Tivoli–Guidonia basin east of Rome (the Bagni di Tivoli
quarries), sawn vein-cut.** Key `stone.travertine-romano`.

*What it is.* Hot springs rising by the Colli Albani laid it down as calcite over the last 115,000 years, crust on
crust, trapping gas as they grew. That is why it has its voids, and why they lie in layers. It is sawn **vein-cut**,
across the bed, so a face shows the layers' edges as long, wavy bands of ivory, cream and honey, with the voids drawn
out along them like small lenses.

*Why this stone.* It is the only stone that is equally at home in both halves of the museum's theme:
- It is **the** Classical stone of Rome: the Colosseum, St Peter's colonnade, the Pantheon's foundations and piers.
- It is also the modern museum stone: the Getty Center is built of it (from Bagni di Tivoli), and at the Musée
  d'Orsay Aulenti chose travertine and Buxy limestone for her interior.

It is warm, so it sits under daylight and under paintings without chilling them. It is porous, so a honed wall
catches shadow in its voids and reads as stone from any distance. It is a real, living trade stone, available in
blocks and slabs today, and it ages well: it has stood two thousand years outdoors in Rome.

*How it is laid.*
- The bands always run **along the direction of travel**: down the Salon's axis, along the Hall of Light, through
  each doorway. A mason lays an enfilade this way, and it makes the line of the building visible underfoot.
- Walls and piers keep their voids **open**.
- Floors, treads and anything walked on are **filled** first (with tinted cement or resin, flush), because open
  voids under feet become dirt traps and break out.
- Neighbouring slabs are different slices of the same bed: the tone carries across a joint, and the bands do not.

*Where.* It is the museum's ground and frame:
- every doorway's threshold and surround;
- the Salon's piers, arches and jambs, and the travertine under every arch;
- the Nymphéas oval's floor, benches and the lily pond's moulded coping with its carved lily frieze;
- the Reserve's pier bases, imposts and long stair;
- the Hall of Light's floor, plinth and stereo stones;
- the Atrium's 6 m base;
- all the stone outside: the façade and portico, the drums of the Rotunda and Élan, the parvis, the copings and the
  kerbs;
- the gravel of the walks, which is the museum's own travertine offcuts, crushed.

*Its measured colour is darker and warmer than the old build's.* The research found that every measured travertine
is below the old build's L\* 86–89, and that polishing darkens and warms it further. The museum will look like
travertine and not like cream paint. This is the single largest change in how the building will read.

**The Rotunda's marble: Crema Marfil Coto, from Monte Coto at Pinoso (Alicante), a selected grade with an open
crackle.** Key `stone.crema-marfil`.

*What it is.* It is an Eocene limestone that takes a polish, sold as marble, as nearly every "cream marble" is. It
formed from nummulite shells and lime mud and was later cut by **stylolites**: jagged seams lined with iron oxides
and clay, grey-brown against the cream, with white calcite veins between them. Seams at many angles close into
cells. That network is exactly what the design calls the *crackle*, found in a real stone rather than drawn.

*Why this stone for the anchor.* Four reasons, each physical:
1. **It is lighter than the travertine and of the same warm hue.** Its maker gives a light reflectance near 64 %,
   against about 40 % for honed travertine. So the Rotunda, the anchor, is the palest stone room in the museum. It
   glows as you come back to it from any wing, while staying in the same family of warm calcite.
2. **It is dense** (0.5 % open porosity against travertine's several per cent), so it takes a true high polish
   without filling. That is what lets the same stone come back at the end of the visit as Élan's glass-smooth
   floor. Travertine cannot do this honestly.
3. **It is the same mineral as the travertine:** calcite, the same refractive index and the same reflectance at the
   face. The two stones meet without the eye seeing two different kinds of matter.
4. **It stays indoors.** It loses about 15 % of its flexural strength after 56 freeze–thaw cycles, and Beijing and
   New York both freeze. So the marble never goes outside, and every exterior surface is travertine.

Botticino Classico, the Italian cream "marble", was considered and set aside: its stylolites run in lines, parallel
to the bed, and never close into cells. It remains the alternative if the owner prefers an Italian stone
(`alternativesConsidered` in the research record).

*Where.*
- The Rotunda's walls, sixteen fluted pilasters and niche surrounds and sills, the sun clock's dial field and outer
  ring, the lifting drum and the spiral stair;
- the Classical Hall's walls below it;
- in the Future, mirror-polished: the Atrium's floor and the Sphere's platform.

The visitor arrives on this stone and ends on it, at the centre of the sky.

**The stone's finish by era.**

| Era | Travertine | Crema Marfil |
|---|---|---|
| Classical | Honed (400–800 grit, satin, no image in it). Open voids on walls, filled on floors. | Honed: a low, even sheen, the crackle soft. |
| Modern | Filled and polished: a soft but clear reflection of the garden and the glass (the Hall of Light). | Absent. The anchor skips the modern. |
| Future | Filled and polished at the Atrium's base: the last of the modern, under the glass. | Mirror-polished (through 3000 grit and a final polishing compound): the Atrium floor and the Sphere platform. |
| Doorways, every era | Honed, always. A threshold is where feet wear any polish off first, so the museum gives every door the finish that feet would leave it anyway. The doorway stands outside time. | — |
| Outdoors | Honed or bush-hammered, then weathered: outdoors, weather sets the finish. | Never outdoors. |

### 2.2 White: lime plaster, then limewash, then pearl steel

The white is always the lightest surface overhead. It changes in its **making**, not only its sheen: the same
calcite as the stone, first trowelled, then brushed, then replaced by a factory coating in the future.

**Lime plaster** (`white.lime-plaster`). Matured lime putty and marble dust, laid in three coats over the masonry.
The setting coat is closed with a steel trowel as it hardens, so it has a faint sheen and a slight undulation of the
hand. The coffer steps and ribs are **run** in place with zinc templates, in lime gauged with plaster of Paris.

*Why.* It is the classical vault material from Vitruvius on. Once carbonated it is calcite, the same mineral as the
stone below it, so the dome and the walls are physically one family. It ages the way real vaults age: dust on the
upward steps, hairlines along the structure, and matched patches.

*Where.*
- The Rotunda's coffered dome, the Salon's coffered barrel vault, and the Classical Hall's vaults and the tribune's
  dome;
- the Nymphéas oval's walls, **burnished** to a soft sheen (a marmorino), so the room at the end is the one pale
  room;
- the Classical Hall's niches, the **same lime pigmented with red ochre**: an iron-oxide red, burnished
  (`white.lime-plaster.red-ochre`: the ochre is in the setting coat's body, so it is a variant of the lime, not a
  working, one key per pigment as the silks are one key per dye).

**Fibrous plaster** (`white.fibrous-plaster`, face `white.plaster-of-paris`). Cast in the workshop: a plaster of
Paris face on hessian and laths, in straight lengths, with the mitres made by hand.

*Where.* The Rotunda's entablature and cornice at the springing, and the Salon's cornice with its cove light. That
is, the mouldings above reach, where lightness matters.

*Changed from the research's first reading:* the niche **sills and surrounds** are Crema Marfil, not plaster. A sill
carries a plaster cast more than two metres tall, and a real builder would bed it on stone.

**Plaster of Paris** (`white.plaster-of-paris`). The four casts in the Rotunda and the 41 in the Classical Hall,
after SMK's Royal Cast Collection. They are cast in piece moulds, **keeping their seam lines**, sealed with a thin
size so they have the slightly waxy sheen of an old cast, and dust-soiled on their upward forms.

**Limewash** (`white.limewash`). Lime putty thinned to milk and brushed on in four coats: dead matt, chalky, with the
brush showing. It is the white's second state, where the museum turns to the garden:
- Chenghuai's garden walls (粉墙), over a paper-fibre lime render;
- the house's interiors, in hemp-fibre lime whitened with whiting (麻刀灰, 大白).

**Pearl steel** (`white.pearl-steel`). The white of the future is made in a factory.
- **The steel:** EN 10025 S355J2 steel, the grade of the Great Court roof.
- **The preparation:** blast-cleaned to Sa 2½ and primed with epoxy.
- **The finish:** an **oyster-white** basecoat (RAL 1013, a white warmed toward the travertine), a **silver-white
  mica interference pearl**, and a satin clear coat. Outdoors the same build is in fluoropolymer.

The pearl is real: titanium-dioxide-coated mica flakes in the coat, which brighten and shift slightly toward
grazing angles. That is what "pearl" means in a paint shop.

*Where.* Every structural white in the museum is this steel:
- the Rotunda's eye lattice;
- the Hall of Light's vault and mullions;
- the Atrium's twelve ribs;
- the Sphere's underside grid, collar and neck, and the iris blades' linings;
- the Élan's outer shell, welded and faired seamless.

*Why the Rotunda's eye is pearl, not the as-built dark grey.* The eye is the museum's seed. The Hall of Light's vault
is "the Rotunda's lattice drawn out", and the Sphere's underside is the same grid again. One steel in one paint makes
that lineage visible from the first step. The precedent says the same: the Great Court's steel is painted a light,
neutral colour so that it recedes against the sky. So the future is already present, quietly, in the anchor's
dome, beside the other "one touch of future", the idealised sun. (This changes an as-built colour and is listed
under open questions.)

**The white's finish by era:** Classical is trowelled or cast, and matt (burnished in the oval). Modern, and the
garden, is limewash, dead matt. Future is pearl, satin.

### 2.3 Bronze: one alloy, CuSn10

**Tin bronze CuSn10 (EN 1982 CuSn10-C, UNS C90700): about 90 copper to 10 tin, lead-free.** Key `bronze.cusn10`.

*Why.* It is the casting bronze of antiquity and of the art foundry. At about 10 % tin it reaches the warm golden
colour everyone means by "bronze". It fills the finest detail of a mould, which the sun clock's numerals and motto
need. It is made both as sand and lost-wax castings and as continuously cast bar, so the inlays, the strips, the
rings and the rails are all literally one metal. Three alternatives were rejected:
- 85-5-5-5 leaded gunmetal: it is tan and contains lead;
- silicon bronze: it is redder;
- "architectural bronze": it is really a brass.

**Its finish by era.**

| Era | Working | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Classical | **Patinated**: a hot sulfide statuary patina, brown to near black, waxed. Relieved where hands and feet polish it. | The sun clock's lines, numerals and motto; the eye's node; the drum's balustrade; the pond's four posts; the Reserve's carriages and globe rods; Albion's electroliers; Chenghuai's fire vats and hardware. |
| Every threshold | **Brushed**: a medium satin, 180–240 grit, grain along the strip, never lacquered, kept bright by feet. | The one metal line every visitor crosses, at every doorway and under every Salon arch. |
| Modern | **Brushed** | The Hall of Light's print stems, stereo readers and glazing bars; handrails; the stele feet; the Reserve's floor tracks. |
| Future | **Mirror-polished and lacquered**, or **gilt** | The ring you wait on, the rib shoes, the collar's band, the car's rings, the mast, the gilt ball; the letters A to K. |

**Gilt** is a working, not a substance (D-014): **23.75 carat gold leaf**, about a tenth of a micron thick.
- **Water-gilt** on gesso over **red bole**: this is the only gilding that burnishes to a mirror. It is used on the
  picture frames, where the bole's iron red glints through on worn ridges.
- **Oil-gilt**: it cannot be burnished, has a soft even lustre, and is robust. It is used on bronze and plaster:
  - the sun clock's meridian and the gilt sun at the centre;
  - the rosettes in the Salon's and the tribune's coffers;
  - the viewing stones' rings and the pond's inner lip;
  - Élan's lines, letters, collar rim, rail, mast and ball.

Where gilt is walked on (the arrival sun, the meridian, the viewing-stone rings), feet wear through the leaf, and
the brown bronze shows where people have stood. Classical gilt is aged: dirt in the hollows, bole on the ridges.
Future gilt is clean and never patinated.

**Brass** (`accent.brass-cuzn30`, cartridge brass CuZn30) is the bronze family's one declared accent in copper
alloy, and it is used only where a wrought alloy is the real material: the Reserve's **woven wire mesh** and the rack
frames around it, which are drawn and rolled forms. Everything cast, walked or large is the bronze. The board's
"brass foot" under the steles and the "brass strip" between oak and stone in the Salon become **bronze**, because
they are a casting and a threshold.

### 2.4 Oak: French sessile oak, quartersawn

***Quercus petraea*, sessile oak from French state forests (the slow-grown oak of the Tronçais forest in the Allier
is the reference), quartersawn for floors and joinery.** Key `oak.sessile-french`.

*Why.* It is the oak of French parquet and French joinery, and the parquet de Versailles is this oak. It also
belongs to the Victorian rooms: English "wainscot" oak was this same sessile oak, quartersawn. Quartersawing does two
things:
- it makes the boards stable, which 1.2 m Versailles panels over a museum's conditioned air need;
- it shows the **ray fleck** (*maillure*), the silvery flakes that no printed wood has and every real oak floor
  shows in raking light.

It ages honestly: tannin and light darken it to honey. Feet wear paths across the wax, and hands polish the edges.

*Conservation rule, museum-wide:* **no bare oak inside a sealed case**, because oak gives off acetic acid. Cases,
plan chests and the 1874 table case have oak outside, and inside the seal their trays and drawers are lined with a
barrier film and acid-free board. Chenghuai's brief already says this, and it now applies everywhere.

**Its finish by era.**
- **Classical:** oiled and hard-waxed (the Salon's parquet and the Manet cabinet's floor, the oval's bench seats, the
  Reserve's easel), or **fumed** with ammonia, whose reaction with the oak's own tannin darkens it right through, and
  then waxed (bench plinths, the 1874 case, the Reserve's plan chests). In Albion it is fumed and
  **French-polished** with shellac, the Victorian finish.
- **Modern:** fumed, **wire-brushed** and oiled. The soft grain is taken down and the hard latewood stands up. It is
  used for the thin dark frames of the Hall of Light's photographs.
- **Future:** absent. The one exception is invisible: the oak core of the Starry Night's slim frame, under clean,
  burnished gold.

**Frames are gilt over oak.** French nineteenth-century frames were carved in oak, gessoed, with composition
ornament, and water-gilt on red bole. Rossetti's and Madox Brown's reeded frames for the Pre-Raphaelites were
**gilded oak**, the grain showing through the gold. So the Salon's frames and Albion's frames are both gilt over the
one oak. This replaces the old build's frames as a bronze material and the Albion sheet's pine.

**What the oak carries** (accents of oak, tied by touch and by value):
- **Silk** on the Salon's piers and walls, woven plain with a soft slub. It is stretched on battens over an
  interlining, as museum wall silks are, in the five chapter colours and the Manet cabinet's green. Each silk sits one
  step deeper than the stone, near the oak's value, so the gilt frames glow against it.
- **Mohair velvet** on the Salon's backless benches, the hard-wearing pile of museum and theatre seats, dyed in each
  bay's own colour.
- **Linen**: the muslin blind in bay 4's lantern, and the Nymphéas oval's velarium, stretched under the laylight like
  the Orangerie's velum.
- **Wool felt** under the Degas pastels, and **wool baize** in Albion's case beds.

## 3. Glass and water

Glass is not one of the four families, but it tells the same story, and it tells it through iron.

- **Low-iron float glass, Pilkington Optiwhite** (`glass.low-iron`), is the museum's glass. It is colourless through
  its face and pale aqua along its edge.
  - It is laminated with a UV-filtering interlayer wherever daylight reaches art, so there are no blinds (D-014):
    the Rotunda's eye, the Salon's lanterns, the Hall of Light, the Sphere's underside.
  - It is anti-reflective in cases.
  - It is **acid-etched** as Élan's **misty glass** and the car's frosted floor. The future's glass is the same glass,
    given the future's finish.
  - In Chenghuai it is laminated with **mulberry paper** (桑皮纸), so the lattice windows glow the way paper windows
    do, with the UV cut.
- **Clear float** (`glass.clear-float`), ordinary soda-lime glass with its green edge, is used only in Albion's
  roofs and screens. A Victorian roof was never water-white, and the iron in its glass is part of the period. It is
  laminated with a UV-filtering interlayer, because Albion's paintings are real.
- **Mouth-blown glass from Glashütte Lamberts** (`glass.mouth-blown`), the German works that still blows flat glass
  for stained glass:
  - **pot-metal and flashed antique glass**, leaded in cames, for Albion's twelve lancets and the Tristram and
    Isolde band;
  - **flashed opal** for the Salon's bay 2 lantern;
  - **cased opal globes** for the Reserve, Albion's electroliers and the lamps of the Grounds.
- **Water** (`water.still`) is still water over a dark bed. The Salon's pond is dyed black, as formal reflecting
  pools are. It and the Grounds' canal lie on **Belgian bluestone** beds, so the surface reflection dominates and
  the water becomes a mirror of the velarium, the panels and the sky.

## 4. The doorway detail

Every doorway in the museum is the same detail, in every era:
- **The threshold:** a single slab of Travertino Romano, vein-cut, **honed**, running the full depth of the wall,
  its bands running through the door.
- **At the seam** between the two stages: a **brushed CuSn10 strip**, set flush, its grain along the strip, never
  lacquered, kept bright by feet.
- **The surround:** a **honed travertine** architrave on both faces.

The same line repeats under each of the Salon's transverse arches, between the oak parquet and the travertine under
the arch. The chapters of the Salon are rooms too. The detail is identical at:
- the Rotunda's four doors and its stair port;
- the Classical Hall's port;
- the Reserve's west door;
- the Hall of Light's two doors;
- the porch into Chenghuai's gate;
- Albion's porch.

It does not follow the era. It is the museum's constant.

## 5. The accents, and how each ties back

An accent is a material the museum needs for one room's truth. Each names the core family it ties back to (D-014),
and it ties back by **value**, by **hue**, and where possible by the **same physics**.

**The Rotunda's dial and the Classical Hall's coloured marbles** (accents of stone). All are calcite stones except the
porphyry, so they wear together with the cream marble underfoot. The Natural Stone Institute warns that stones of
unequal hardness in one floor wear unevenly.
- **Rojo Alicante** (`accent.rojo-alicante`), from La Romana, the next village to Pinoso: a micritic limestone,
  iron-red with white calcite veins. It is the dial's red band, and, below, the Classical Hall's dado. So the red you
  cross on the dial is the red you find under the casts. It replaces Rosso Levanto, a serpentinite that is harder
  than the calcite around it and would stand proud as the dial wore.
- **Negro Marquina** (`accent.negro-marquina`), from Markina in Bizkaia: a black limestone with white calcite veins.
  It is the dial's black band, and it is kept. Crema Marfil, Rojo Alicante and Negro Marquina are the classic Spanish
  triad; two of them come from neighbouring quarries.
- **Giallo di Siena** (`accent.giallo-siena`), from the Montagnola Senese: the yellow marble the Renaissance used
  once the Roman *giallo antico* of Chemtou was exhausted. It is the gallery's paired Ionic shafts and the floor's
  squares.
- **Pavonazzetto** (`accent.pavonazzetto`), today's violet-veined white marble from the Afyon–İscehisar district,
  where the ancient quarries of Docimium were: the source of the Romans' own pavonazzetto. It is the tribune's twelve
  columns.
- **Verde antico** (`accent.verde-antico`), the serpentine breccia of Thessaly near Larissa, still quarried where the
  Romans quarried it: the floor's bands and panels.
- **Imperial porphyry** (`accent.porphyry`), from Mons Porphyrites in Egypt, exists today only as reclaimed stone. The
  roundels of the Pantheon's floor and of every Cosmati pavement are slices of ancient columns, so here too the
  roundels are **reclaimed**, the one stone in the museum that could only be found, never quarried.
- **Statuario from Carrara** (`accent.statuario`): honed, for the carved bases, capitals and entablature, and the
  white floor fields.

These are accents of stone by **hue**: straw, violet, green and red sit around the cream marble the way they sit round Carrara in the Pantheon and the Braccio Nuovo, whose columns are giallo antico, cipollino, alabaster and granite.

**Belgian bluestone** (`accent.pierre-bleue`), the Petit Granit of Soignies (Hainaut). It is a dark, crinoidal
limestone, and it is the Paris stone for steps, sills and cellar floors. It is used, honed, for the Reserve's floor
and for the beds of the pond, the basin and the canal. It ties to the stone family by **physics**: it is calcite
again, the travertine's dark partner. It replaces the old build's dark polished concrete in the Reserve, which read
modern under a Paris salon.

**Fired clay: the stone that is made.** Four accents of stone that are all fired clay, and whose colour is the iron
in the clay and the air in the kiln:
- **Burgundy pressed brick**, *brique de Bourgogne* (`accent.brique-bourgogne`): the pale buff-pink brick that
  vaulted Paris's cellars and reservoirs in the 1860s–1900s. Montsouris (1874) is the precedent. Today it is reclaimed
  or matched from a Burgundy works. It is the Reserve's piers, arches and groin vaults, in a lime mortar. It replaces
  the anonymous "buff stock" with the brick that actually lies under Paris, and it sits at the travertine's hue a
  step deeper.
- **Encaustic tile** (`accent.encaustic-tile`), in red, buff and black after Pugin and Minton's floors for
  Westminster, as made today by Craven Dunnill Jackfield: Albion's porch and nave floors.
- **Grey brick** (`accent.grey-brick`): the Ming and Qing wall brick that the Linqing kilns sent up the Grand Canal
  to Beijing, fired and then **quenched with water in the sealed kiln**. The iron in the clay is reduced instead of
  oxidised, so the brick comes out blue-grey instead of red. It is Chenghuai's walls:
  - rubbed and dry-laid (干摆) at the gate, the spirit wall and the hall's base;
  - hairline-jointed (丝缝) on the halls;
  - lime-pointed (淌白) at the back;
  - on edge (城砖) in the court paths.
- **Square floor tiles from the Suzhou imperial kilns at Lumu** (`accent.suzhou-fangzhuan`), the *jinzhuan* that
  floored the Forbidden City: 54 cm in the main hall, soaked in tung oil (钻生) to a dark satin; 45 cm with ground
  joints (细墁) elsewhere.
- **Grey roof tiles** (`accent.bu-wa`), unglazed and reduction-fired like the brick: barrel tiles (筒瓦) on the gate,
  the main hall and the festooned gate, and butterfly tiles (合瓦) elsewhere.

Chenghuai's grey ties back by **hue and by physics**. It is the same iron and clay that make the grey-brown
stylolites in the Rotunda's Crema Marfil: the veins of the anchor, fired. Its red counterpart, the red of oxidised
iron, is all over the museum.

**Chenghuai's stone and garden** (accents of stone):
- **青白石, bluish-white marble from Dashiwo, Fangshan** (`accent.qingbaishi`): the quarry of the Forbidden City's
  terraces. It is the plinth edges, steps, column bases and the gate's 门墩, axe-dressed and honed. It is Beijing's
  anchor stone, and it answers the Rotunda's marble by **value**: the palest stone in its wing.
- **Taihu stone from Lake Tai** (`accent.taihu-rock`): the standing rock 石兄 and the rockery peaks, calcite
  dissolved by water. It is travertine's opposite: stone that water took away, where travertine is stone that water
  laid down.
- **青石, blue-grey limestone** (`accent.qingshi`): the rockery's body, the twelve 书条石 cut after the *Chunhua ge
  tie*, and the ice-crack floors of the garden buildings. It is the Chinese kin of the Belgian bluestone.
- **Pebble mosaic, 花街铺地** (`accent.pebble-mosaic`): river pebbles, tile shards and brick on edge, set in patterns.

**Chenghuai's timber and lacquer** (accents of oak). A Beijing house is not built of oak, and building it of oak would
be a lie. So the board's species stay, and they tie back to the oak by **touch, value and hue**:
- **Korean pine, 红松** (`accent.qing-pine-lacquered`), the late-Qing structural timber of Beijing: columns, beams and
  every lattice.
  - It is laid over a 一麻五灰 ground (hemp and five coats of blood-lime and brick-dust putty).
  - It is then finished in **铁红**, iron-oxide red in tung oil, a dull red: the same iron red as the gilder's bole and
    Rojo Alicante. The gate leaves are **black lacquer**.
  - The house beams carry 苏式彩画 in azurite, malachite and gold leaf, which is the gilt working again.
- **Chinese fir, 杉木, under 广漆 chestnut lacquer** (`accent.chinese-fir-guangqi`), raw urushi with tung oil: all
  the garden timber, the Suzhou brown. It sits at fumed oak's value and hue, translucent over the grain the way the
  wax is over the oak.
- **Phoebe, 楠木, plantation-grown** (`accent.phoebe`): the main hall's carved screens and every case plinth, waxed,
  never painted. Its golden, silky grain is the natural oak's counterpart.

**Albion** (England, 1848–1898). Its stones tie to the travertine by value and hue, and its metals to the bronze:
- **Bath stone** (`accent.bath-stone`), the oolitic limestone of the Monks Park mine: cream to honey, like a quieter
  travertine. It is the ashlar, the rubbed dressings and the carved capitals, with soot in the interior and weather
  outside.
- **Mansfield red sandstone** (`accent.mansfield-red`), Nottinghamshire: the red bands every 1.2 m. It is iron red
  again.
- **York stone flags** (`accent.york-stone`), sawn, trodden, in the aisles, and **Welsh slate from Penrhyn**
  (`accent.welsh-slate`), honed, in the skirting.
- **Ten polished British stones** for the column shafts (`accent.british-column-stones`), as at the Oxford University
  Museum, whose 126 columns each show a different British rock: Lizard serpentine, Peterhead and Rubislaw granite,
  Purbeck, Frosterley, Ashburton, Iona, Tiree and Mona marbles, and Hopton Wood stone.
- **Iron** (`accent.victorian-iron`): cast-iron clustered shafts with cast foliage, wrought-iron rings and saddle
  bars. They are painted as Skidmore painted the Oxford Museum's metal, **in the colour of its own oxide**: a deep
  iron-oxide brown-red. That ties Albion's iron to the patinated bronze by **value** and to the iron-red thread by
  **hue**, instead of the as-built blue-grey, which tied to nothing. (Listed under open questions.)
- **Milled lead** (`accent.milled-lead`): gutters, ridge rolls and the porch flat.
- **Stained glass:** the Lamberts pot-metal glass above. It ties back to the Salon's chapter silks by **hue**: the
  same deep, saturated colours, lit from behind.

**Élan.** It needs no accent at all. Its pearl **is** the white family, its mirror **is** the Rotunda's marble
polished to its limit, and its gilt **is** the bronze's working. The future is made only of the core families, at
their last finish. That is the argument of the whole museum.

## 7. What changed from the old museum

The old museum (`windows/Scripts/materials.py`, the LANGUAGE, NAMED, PHYSICS and POLISH tables, and the
MaterialsStone and MaterialsOther boards) already had the right idea: four families, one finish per era, and the
Rotunda as the anchor. Its weakness was that it chose **looks**, not materials. The colours were set by eye against
renderings; the images were procedural textures or unnamed photographs; the crackle was drawn. It made the right
choices, and now each has a real material behind it. None of its values are carried over (D-018).

| Old choice | What it was trying to do | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| `M_Travertine_Honed` (#E1D6C4, ambientCG Travertine009) on walls, piers, surrounds | The museum's warm, honed stone | **Refined** | Same idea, now a named stone (Travertino Romano, vein-cut). The image was procedural, not travertine, and the colour lighter than any measured travertine. |
| `M_Travertine_Polished` on floors (Salon, oval, Hall of Light, Sculpture Hall) | Modern soft reflection | **Refined** | Kept for the Hall of Light and the Atrium's base. The Salon now has oak with honed travertine under the arches, and the oval honed: Classical is honed. |
| `M_Travertine_Ashlar` (the Sculpture Hall) | Vein-cut ashlar | **Retired** with the Sculpture Hall | Its vein-cut idea is now the rule everywhere. |
| `M_Marble_Wall`: a generated crackle, then Marble014 | The Rotunda's cream crackle marble | **Refined** | The look is kept; the stone is now Crema Marfil, whose stylolite network *is* a crackle. The drawn crackle had read as cracked paint. |
| `M_SunClock_Marble`/`Alt`: statuary white (#EEECE7) | A pale, quiet dial under the bronze lines | **Replaced** | The dial is now the anchor's own Crema Marfil, honed, in two selections. One stone for the anchor; honed so the shadow reads without glare. |
| `M_Marble_Rosso`: Rosso Levanto | The dial's red band | **Replaced** by Rojo Alicante | Rosso Levanto is a serpentinite, harder than the calcite around it, so it would stand proud as the floor wore. Rojo Alicante is calcite and comes from the quarry next door to the Crema Marfil. |
| `M_Marble_Nero`: Nero Marquina | The dial's black band | **Kept** | A calcite stone; wears with the rest. |
| `M_SunClock_Drum` | The drum in the Rotunda's marble | **Kept** (as Crema Marfil, honed) | |
| `M_Elan_Mirror`: "the Rotunda's marble, glass-smooth" | The future's floor returns to the anchor | **Kept**, now real | Crema Marfil is dense enough to mirror-polish honestly. |
| `M_SquareFloor`, `M_SquareFloorDark`, `M_RammedEarth`, `M_Strata` | The Square (level −1) | **Retired** | The Square is replaced by the Cube (D-001). The dark bed idea goes to the pierre bleue. |

## 8. Research: what exists and what is still needed

The records written so far (`r-matter`) cover:
- `travertine`;
- `cream-marble` (Crema Marfil, with Botticino kept as the alternative);
- `lime-plaster`, `fibrous-plaster`, `plaster-of-paris` and `limewash`;
- `low-iron-glass`;
- `bronze`, `brass` and `steel`;
- the working `gilt`;
- their workings and histories.

Two differences between the palette and those records need to be settled:
- **`steel`** is declared there as an accent of **glass**. The palette makes the painted steel the white family's
  third member, **pearl steel**, as the owner's principles name it. The steel record should become the substrate of
  `white.pearl-steel`, and the pearl coating needs its own working (a mica interference coat; the old RAL 1013 is a
  design value).
- **`bronze`** lists "patinated" for the dial's inlays and "brushed" for the collar and car rings. The palette puts
  the Élan's rings at **mirror-polished or gilt**, the future's finish.

Still needed, as records:
- the stones: Rojo Alicante, Negro Marquina, Giallo di Siena, Pavonazzetto, verde antico, porphyry, Statuario,
  Belgian bluestone, Bath stone, Mansfield red, York stone, Welsh slate, the ten British column stones, 青白石, Taihu
  stone, 青石, and pebbles;
- the fired clays: Burgundy brick, encaustic tile, Linqing grey brick, Suzhou fangzhuan, 布瓦;
- the oak, and its workings: oiled and waxed, fumed, French-polished, wire-brushed; also water gilding on oak;
- the Chinese timbers and lacquers: Korean pine with 地仗 and 铁红, Chinese fir with 广漆, phoebe;
- the textiles: silk, mohair velvet, linen, wool felt and baize;
- the other glasses: ordinary clear float, and Lamberts' pot-metal, flashed and opal glass;
- the other metals: Victorian cast and wrought iron with oxide paint, milled lead;
- the rest: concrete, the site geology, and still water with pond dye.

Each needs, as the existing records do, its formation, optics, stock and ageing from sources, and a tier (b)
ColorChecker specimen where colour matters.
