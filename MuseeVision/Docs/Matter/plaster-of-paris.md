# Plaster of Paris (cast plaster)

**Key:** `plaster-of-paris` · **Family:** white · **Records:**
- the substance: `Data/Matter/Substances/plaster-of-paris.json`;
- its working: `cast.plaster`, in `Data/Matter/Workings/`;
- its histories: `white.soiling`, `white.micro-cracking` and `white.patching`, in `Data/Matter/Histories/`.

Plaster of Paris is the substance of the four casts in the Rotunda's niches, and of the face of every fibrous plaster
moulding (`fibrous-plaster` names it as its face). It is gypsum that has been burnt and then set again. It takes a
mould's finest detail, which is why the great nineteenth-century cast collections (the V&A's Cast Courts, the Royal
Cast Collection in Copenhagen) are made of it. It is also soft, porous, slightly soluble, and very hard to clean once
dirty. Numbers in brackets refer to the sources at the end.

## How it is made

**Burning.** Gypsum rock (CaSO4·2H2O) is heated until it gives up three quarters of its water and becomes the
hemihydrate, CaSO4·½H2O. The trade gives about 150 °C for this [1]. The first calcination can start at about
100 °C [2]. In ground natural gypsum, combined water starts to leave above 50 °C, three quarters of it is gone by
180 °C, and all of it only above 400 °C [3]. A finished cast is safe to dry up to 49 °C; above that it begins to
calcine again [4].

**Setting.** The hemihydrate is strewn into water and mixed. It dissolves and recrystallises as a felt of interlocking
gypsum crystals: CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O. Trade plasters set in 10-20 minutes [1]. USG's moulding plaster
has a Vicat set of 25-35 minutes [5]. Unlike lime, gypsum expands slightly as it sets, by 0.15-0.21 % [5][4]. The
expansion pushes the plaster into every detail of the mould.

**Why it is porous.** The reaction needs only 0.186 kg of water per kilogram of hemihydrate (from the formula and the
IUPAC atomic weights [6]). The trade mixes 0.60-0.70 kg [4][5]. The surplus water leaves pores behind when it dries.
A dry casting plaster weighs 1153 kg/m³ [4], and gypsum itself 2317 kg/m³ [7], so a set cast is about half void
(1 − 1153/2317 = 0.50). It is weak in compression (11.7-17.2 MPa dry [5]), scratched by a fingernail (Mohs 1.5-2
[7]) and slightly soluble. Published solubilities in pure water at 25 °C run from 0.0147 to 0.0182 mol/L, that is
2.5-3.1 g of gypsum per litre [8]. Water marks it and running water erodes it.

**Its optics.** Gypsum's refractive indices are 1.521, 1.523 and 1.530 [7], so a facet's specular F0 is 0.043-0.044
(Fresnel, [9]). The white comes from scattering at crystal and pore boundaries in a half-void body, as in lime.
Measured anchors:
- Ground natural white gypsum (USG Terra Alba No. 1) measures L = 88.0, a = 0.8, b = 4.3 on a HunterLab
  colorimeter [3]. The sheet does not name the scale; read as Hunter L (its unstarred notation) that is a luminous
  reflectance of (88.0/100)² = 0.77 [10], and read as CIE L* it would be 0.72. Either way it has a faint warm cast
  from impurities in the rock.
- Plaster of Paris set in water was studied as a field reflectance standard for its high reflectance, fairly flat
  spectrum and nearly perfect diffuseness [11]. The abstract gives no number.

A clean cast of high-purity casting plaster is whiter than natural rock, but no measured value was found. The record
keeps 0.80-0.92 as unverified until the tier (b) specimen settles it. How far light travels inside the plaster before
it comes out is also unmeasured. That distance decides how soft a cast's edges and fingers look, so it matters.

## How it is worked

**Cast in plaster** (`cast.plaster`). A sculpture is cast in a **piece mould**: many small keyed pieces (tasselli),
oiled and shellacked, held together in a plaster mother mould. A fine creamy plaster is run round the inside first,
then the mould is filled with coarser plaster, and it is opened while the cast is set but still damp. Limbs are cast
round iron rods, and separately cast parts are joined with steel dowels and fresh plaster [2]. Where the pieces of the
mould met, the cast carries a **network of fine raised seam lines**. Brigham (1874) valued them as proof of a good
mould: fine lines with sharp intersections mean a mould in good condition, and thick ridges mean a worn one. Casts
were normally sent with their seams [2]. Careless pouring traps air (pinholes). A jet of slurry striking the mould
face makes a denser hard spot that absorbs differently [4].

**Finishes.** Few nineteenth-century casts were left bare:
- They were sealed with shellac, size or dextrine to cut porosity and ease cleaning [2].
- The Perry casts (South Kensington, later the British Museum) carry a thin barium- and lead-rich hardening layer,
  then lead paint warmed with earth pigments, then later "refreshing" coats. At most four layers were found, 40 to
  280 µm in all [2].
- Store casts coated in shellac are now dark brown [2].
- V&A casts were finished to imitate bronze, ivory, stone, wood and gilding [12].

The Rotunda's four casts may be bare or sealed. The choice is a making-time decision on the piece, and the working
record lists both.

## How it ages

- **Soiling** (`white.soiling`). Dust settles thickest on upward forms. The V&A's 1856 plaster David had a thick
  layer on its shoulders and head, and 1920s photographs show it "rather grubby" from coal heating and gas
  lighting [13]. On the monumental casts, dust settled on the surface can form a damaging "cement", and the soiling
  is heavy and preferential [14]. Porous plaster is discoloured by dirt penetrating it [2], and soot in pores is
  extremely hard, often impossible, to remove [15]. Grimed casts were cleaned and some were given a further
  "refreshing" coat of paint [2].
- **Micro-cracking** (`white.micro-cracking`). Casts crack if dried too fast [2]. Humidity is carried through the
  plaster to iron armatures, which corrode [14]. On a cast that was painted and waxed, the old paint cracks, peels and
  flakes [13].
- **Patching** (`white.patching`). Slender parts drop off fresh casts and are replaced [2]. Old casts carry historic
  repairs, dents and hairline cracks [13], and some carry repainting [2].

## What to look for in a photograph

- The seam-line network along limbs and drapery. It is the surest sign of a cast.
- A matt, chalky white when bare, softly scattering. Sealed or waxed casts show a low sheen and a warmer, sometimes
  browner tone.
- Pinholes and hard spots under raking light on broad smooth areas.
- Grey dust on heads, shoulders and the tops of folds, and old joins at fingers and attributes.
