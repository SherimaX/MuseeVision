# Limewash

**Key:** `limewash` · **Family:** white · **Records:**
- the substance: `Data/Matter/Substances/limewash.json`;
- the working that lays it: `limewashed`, in `Data/Matter/Workings/`;
- its histories: `white.soiling`, `white.micro-cracking` and `white.patching`, in `Data/Matter/Histories/`.

Limewash is the oldest white paint. It is lime putty thinned with water to the consistency of milk and brushed onto a
damp wall in several very thin coats [1][2]. It is a substance in its own right, and the working `limewashed` lays it
over another substance: in this museum, over lime plaster. It is matt, breathable and renewable. It wears away by
chalking and is renewed rather than repaired. Numbers in brackets refer to the sources at the end.

## How it is made

Matured lime putty (calcium hydroxide) is diluted with water. Pigment, if any, is first mixed into hot water [1].
Additives can be used, but each changes it:
- **tallow** or **linseed oil** for water shedding; tallow works only if it goes in during slaking [2];
- **casein** for binding;
- **common salt**, to reduce dusting [2].

SPAB warns that extra binders such as tallow and linseed oil reduce breathability [1]. Plain limewash has
at least ten times the vapour permeability of modern resin paint [3].

On the wall the film loses water and carbonates to calcite: Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3 + H2O. Carbonation is slow in cold
or humid weather [2]. While wet the film is semi-transparent. It dries opaque and many shades lighter [3][2], because
air replaces water between the fine particles and the index contrast, and so the scattering, rises. For the same
reason it darkens again for a while after rain [1].

**Its optics.** The wet film is portlandite (n = 1.547-1.575) and the dry film calcite (n = 1.486-1.658) [4]. The facet
F0 is 0.046-0.050 wet and 0.038-0.061 carbonated (Fresnel, [5]). The film cannot be whiter than fine white calcium
carbonate powder, which measures a luminous reflectance of 0.95 [6]. A fresh white limewash has not been measured
here; the record keeps 0.80-0.93 as unverified. One coat does not hide its ground, which is why three or four are
built up.

## How it is worked

**Limewashed** (`limewashed`):
1. The surface is wetted with clean cold water and left until damp but not wet [2].
2. The limewash is scrubbed in thinly with a large, rough brush, in a circular motion [1][2].
3. At least three or four coats are built up. Each is left to dry and the surface is dampened before the next [1].

Laid thin, it carbonates to a sound skin. Laid liberally, it crazes [2]. It is a continuous finish with no bays or
joints. Over a lime finish gauged with gypsum, the different suction changes how the limewash looks [7].

## How it ages

- **Soiling** (`white.soiling`). It greys evenly. Outdoors it also chalks and streaks. It is renewed rather than
  cleaned.
- **Renewal** (`white.patching`). Externally it is typically renewed about every five years [1]. Each campaign adds
  thin layers, so an old wall's arrises and fine relief soften under generations of coats. The indoor interval was
  not found.
- **Crazing** (`white.micro-cracking`). A fine craze appears where a coat went on thick [2].

## What to look for in a photograph

- A dead-matt, slightly uneven, chalky white. The brushwork shows, and so does the texture of the ground beneath.
- Thin places where the ground glimmers through, and thicker places with a fine craze.
- Softened arrises and filled detail on long-limewashed work.
- Outdoors, darker while wet after rain, and chalk streaks below ledges.
