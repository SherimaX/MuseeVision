# Fibrous plaster (moulding plaster)

**Key:** `fibrous-plaster` · **Family:** white · **Face:** `plaster-of-paris` · **Records:**
- the substance: `Data/Matter/Substances/fibrous-plaster.json`;
- its workings: `run` and `cast.plaster`, in `Data/Matter/Workings/`;
- its histories: `white.soiling`, `white.micro-cracking` and `white.patching`, in `Data/Matter/Histories/`.

Fibrous plaster is the Rotunda's mouldings: the entablature at the springing, the archivolts, imposts, niche surrounds
and sills. It is a laminate made in the workshop:
- a face coat of plaster of Paris cast against a mould;
- behind it, layers of hessian (jute scrim) soaked in plaster;
- timber laths and ribs bedded in to stiffen the back.

What a visitor sees is only the face coat, so the record names `plaster-of-paris` as its **face** and does not repeat
its optics. This record holds what the laminate adds: reinforcement, stock, fixing and failure. Numbers in brackets
refer to the sources at the end.

## How it is made

**The method.** Reinforcing gypsum with fabric is old. The modern method was patented in 1856 by the French modeller
Leonard Alexander Desachy, drawing on a Parisian recipe [1][2]. It let cornices and ornament be cast in a workshop
and fixed on site, instead of being run and modelled in place. Lighter than solid plaster, easily reinforced and cheap
to make in ornate designs, it quickly became popular with homeowners, theatre and music hall owners, and
architects [2].

**The model and the mould.** A profile is **run** on the bench: a zinc template on a wooden horse is drawn along rules
through setting plaster until the full section stands [3][4]. A mould is taken from that model, or from a modelled
original. It is a layer of flexible compound backed with plaster and a framework, so that the mould keeps its
geometry [1].

**The cast.** A face coat of plaster about 6 mm (¼ inch) deep goes into the mould. Hessian dipped in plaster is laid
behind it in layers, and sawn laths and ribs are bedded in [5][1]. When set, the length is lifted out. A cornice comes
in straight lengths, typically 3 m [6]. On site the lengths are fixed with nails, screws or metalwork, or by **wads**:
strips of plaster-soaked hessian tied round the structure above [2]. Sections are cut for corners, mitres and
returns, which are made up and finished by hand [7][4].

**The matrix.** The plaster is moulding-grade plaster of Paris, mixed at 0.63-0.70 kg of water per kilogram. It sets
with a slight expansion of 0.15-0.20 % [8], so it does not shrink-craze as lime does. In the laboratory,
high-density gypsum took up 0.2 % moisture and low-density gypsum 1 %, while the jute of the hessian took up more
than 20 % [9]. The hessian is the part that suffers from damp.

## How it is worked

- **Run** (`run`). The profile of every length is a template's, so it is exactly constant along the member. The zinc
  edge forms crisp arrises, and grit dragged by the template leaves fine striations along the run [4].
- **Cast in plaster** (`cast.plaster`). The face is a replica of the mould's face: fine, matt, with pinholes where air
  lay against the mould. Enrichments (repeated ornament) are cast separately in moulds and fixed to the run [7].

## How it ages

- **Fixings** (`white.micro-cracking`). Hessian scrim has a normal life of about 80 years [1][2]. A wad can look sound
  while the hessian inside has decayed, leaving an "empty egg shell" of plaster. The Apollo Theatre ceiling in London
  fell in December 2013 when its hessian ties failed [2]. Hairline cracks appear along the joints between lengths and
  at fixings.
- **Soiling** (`white.soiling`). Dust lies on the upward faces of cornices, sills and imposts, and in quirks and
  fillets.
- **Repair** (`white.patching`). Failed plaster is cleaned off and the ties re-wadded. Weak areas are backed with new
  hessian stuck on with casting plaster. Glass-reinforced gypsum is substituted for extensive losses [1].

## What to look for in a photograph

- Long straight members with a perfectly constant profile, broken about every 3 m by the joint between two cast
  lengths.
- Mitres and returns that are slightly softer and less regular than the runs, because they were made by hand.
- The fine, matt face of plaster of Paris, sometimes with pinholes.
- Dust on the upper faces. In old work, sagging or cracking where wads have failed.
