# Antique glass: Victorian stained and sheet glass

  - `leaded`, which glazes the pieces in lead came;

This is the glass of Albion:
- **The Tristram and Isolde band** in the south screen: twelve panels, 9.0 to 12.6 m up.
- **The twelve lancets.** On the west, Philip Webb's birds and Morris's flowers in diamond quarries. On the east,
  Chaucer's Good Women after Burne-Jones.
- **The clear glass on iron bars** of the three vaults and the end screens.

The band's panels are Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co.'s of 1862, made for Harden Grange and now at Bradford Museums.
Each original is about 68 × 60.5 cm; here they are enlarged about two and a half times to 1.6 × 1.7 m [13].

## What it is

It is soda-lime glass, the same chemical family as today's float. In 1911 "good sheet-glass" was about 72 % silica, 13 %
lime and 14 % soda, with iron and alumina together about 1 % [1]. Soda-lime glass of the 19th century carries 13 to 17 %
soda, and its higher silica makes it far more durable than medieval potash glass [2].

What separates it from modern float is how it was made.

**Coloured in the pot** ("pot-metal"). A metal oxide dissolved in the melt colours the whole thickness [1]:
- cobalt gives purple-blue;
- chromium, green or yellow;
- manganese, violet;
- ferrous iron, olive green or pale blue;
- copper, peacock blue;
- copper or gold, ruby.

**Clear sheet** ("white" in the trade) keeps the faint green of its iron.

**Flashed.** Ruby in its full thickness is nearly black, so it is flashed: "ordinary white glass covered upon one side
with a thin coating of intensely coloured glass" [1].

## How it is made

**Mouth-blown cylinder** (`blown.cylinder`) [3], [1]:
1. The glassmaker gathers glass from the pot, which today is melted at 1450 °C for 14 hours.
2. He inflates it to a balloon, turning it in a mould that gives the surface its structure.
3. He swings it out into a cylinder, opens the end and knocks off the pipe.
4. When the cylinder is cool, it is cut along its length, reheated, laid open and flattened with a piece of wood.

Each sheet is about 60 × 90 cm and about 3 mm thick. In fact it runs from about 2 mm in the middle to 5 mm near the
natural edge [3], [4]. It keeps the marks of its making:
- **seeds** (bubbles), which makers set to order from none to strong;
- **reams and cords;**
- **a slow waviness** [4], [5].

**Rolled plate** (`rolled.glass`). James Hartley's rolled plate, of about 1847, was ladled onto an iron table and rolled
[1], [6]. It roofed stations and greenhouses. Rough-cast plate was made ¼ to 1 inch thick [7]. The Oxford Museum's roof
is of this family [8], [9]:
- about 8,500 diamond-shaped panels of sand-cast glass;
- cast to a nominal 10 mm, but really 8 to 12 mm;
- each with its own pattern.

In the 2011-2014 restoration every panel was removed, cleaned and reset. Broken ones were replaced by kiln-slumped glass
moulded from the best originals.

**Which glass for the vaults?** The Albion board says "clear glass on iron bars", which is cylinder sheet. Oxford itself
used cast glass. The records carry both, and the choice is the Architect's.

## What light does in it

**Index and reflection.** No historic sheet was measured here. A modern soda-lime glass of the same class stands in:
- n = 1.523 (the low-iron record);
- n_e = 1.525 (Schott's clear drawn base glass) [10].

Each face then reflects about 4.3 % at normal incidence, derived here.

**Pot-metal colour is absorption, not tint.** Transmittance falls as exp(−α d) with the thickness d. An antique sheet
2 mm thick in the middle and 5 mm at its edge is deeper in colour where it is thick. That variation, with the seeds and
reams, is what glaziers call the glass's "movement". Matter should therefore carry each colour as an absorption per
millimetre and let Form give each piece its thickness, rather than one flat RGB. The low-iron record already carries
this form for iron.

**Flashed colour** sits in a thin skin, so a flashed ruby is even in depth whatever the sheet's thickness does. It pales
only where the flash thins.

**Clear sheet.** The Victorian "white" has more iron than a low-iron float. The low-iron record's Vogt et al. series
(up to 703 ppm Fe₂O₃) is the physics to use, once the iron content is set. That content is unverified; it is probably
0.1-0.5 % Fe₂O₃.

**The as-built palette.** It is recorded as design values: sRGB colours "as transmitted light reads" in the
photographs, with exposure unknown [11], [12]. They are Art's colours for the atlases. They are not spectra, and they
must not be read as absorption.

## How it is worked

**Painted** (`painted.vitreous`) [14]. The paint is finely ground low-melting glass with iron or copper oxide, laid on
with water or vinegar and gum arabic:
1. the trace lines first, near-opaque;
2. then mattes, stippled, badgered or scratched out to model the light.

It is fired at 650-700 °C into "a thin (10-100 µm) and uneven layer". Morris's firm drew with brown-black grisaille from
cartoons whose leads were marked in brown. One mid-century paint analysed was about 50 % pigment by weight, in a hard
lead-silicate flux (lead to silica about 3:2), with coarse iron oxide [15].

**Silver stain** (`silver-stained`) [16]. A silver compound is brushed on the back and fired. Silver ions exchange into
the glass and grow into nanoparticles, from a few to about 100 nm, in a layer 10-20 µm deep. Their plasmon absorbs blue,
so the glass transmits yellow, deepening to amber. One piece of white glass can therefore be clear, lemon and amber at
once. That is how Morris's quarries get their yellow flowers and the band its figures' hair. The technique has been
used since the early 14th century [2].

**Leaded** (`leaded`):
1. The glazier cuts each piece to the cartoon's cutline.
2. He fits the pieces into opened H-section came, cutting and shaping the came around each one.
3. He solders every junction on both faces.
4. He cements the panel under the leaves to seal and stiffen it.
5. In the window, copper ties fix the panels to iron saddle bars.

The came is the drawing's black line: 6 mm in the as-built atlases [11], within the maker's 3-25 mm range [17]. Came is
described in the `lead` dossier.

## How it ages

**Paint loss** (`glass.paint-loss`) [15]. Much mid- and late-19th-century paint was badly fused and has flaked or washed
away. What is lost first:
- faces, hands and inscriptions, which survive as "ghost images";
- anything on the inside face, which is wet with condensation "on a daily cycle";
- paint on clear glass.

Back-painting and paint on the outside of yellow pot-metal survive best. Hardman's Sherborne windows of 1851 had failed
within about 14 years. Several firms were affected; Clayton & Bell, and Heaton Butler & Bayne, less so. Whether
Morris's 1862 panels have lost paint was not read. How much loss the band shows is a design decision, and is listed
as unverified.

**Weathering** (`glass.corrosion`) [2]. Soda-lime Victorian glass is durable, so its windows show soiling rather than
crusts. Pits run from 0.1 mm to 2-4 mm, and about 2 mm has been seen on flashed ruby.

**Soiling.** The London grime on the roof glass follows the existing `glass.soiling`. Albion's weather is London's
(D-005).

## What to look for in a photograph

- Colour that varies within one piece, seeds as tiny bright points, reams as faint streaks.
- Crisp trace lines, a granular brown haze of matting, and silver-stain yellow inside a white piece.
- Reflections breaking at every lead, because each piece sits at its own angle.
- The clear sheet: faint green, the sky's lines bent by a slow waviness, and soot at the pane edges.
