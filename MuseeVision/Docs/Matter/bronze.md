# Bronze: tin bronze CuSn10, the museum's one bronze

**Key:** `bronze` · **Family:** bronze · **Records:**
- the substance: `Data/Matter/Substances/bronze.json`;
- its workings: `cast.bronze`, `brushed`, `patinated` and `gilt` (gold leaf laid over it), in `Data/Matter/Workings/`;
- its histories: `bronze.tarnish`, `bronze.wear`, `bronze.weathering` and `gilt.wear`, in `Data/Matter/Histories/`.

The museum has one bronze (`Matter.OneLanguage`). It appears in these places:
- the brushed strip of every doorway, lying on the seam between two stages;
- the ring of every well collar;
- the sun clock's inlaid lines, ticks, numerals and motto in the polished marble dial;
- the node at the centre of the Rotunda's eye lattice;
- the slim rings of the Élan car.

Brass appears too, but only as the bronze family's declared accent (see `brass.md`). Numbers in brackets refer to the sources at the end. Every number is also in the JSON record, next to its unit, its grade and its source.

## Which bronze, and why

The alloy is **CuSn10**, a tin bronze of about 90 parts copper to 10 parts tin. Its designations are:
- EN 1982 CuSn10-C, CC480K;
- UNS C90700;
- BS 1400 CT1 [1].

Its limits are:

| Element | Share (wt%) |
|---|---|
| Cu | 88.0–90.0 |
| Sn | 9.0–11.0 |
| Pb | ≤ 1.0 |
| Zn | ≤ 0.5 |
| Ni | ≤ 2.0 |
| P, Fe | ≤ 0.2 each |

The traces are held lower still [1].

A founder and a conservator would choose it for these reasons:
- **It is the classical casting bronze.** "Classic bronze", about 10 % tin, was the casting alloy of antiquity; "mild bronze", about 6 %, was hammered [2]. Art foundries still cast statuary in tin bronzes around 90/10 [3].
- **Its colour is the bronze colour.** Tin takes the red out of copper and adds yellow. From about 4 % tin the metal grows yellower, and it reaches its warm golden colour around 9 % [4]. Metalworkers describe 10 % tin, polished, as "distinctly golden (typical bronze color)", with good casting qualities [4].
- **It fills the mould.** Bronze expands slightly just before it sets, so it takes the finest detail. This matters for the numerals and letters of the dial [2].
- **One alloy serves every piece.** CuSn10-C is made as foundry castings and also as centrifugal and continuously cast bar [1]. The inlays can be cast, and the strips, rings and rails cut from bar of the same metal. The Copper Development Association warns that most alloys are not made in every form, so colour-matched work usually needs several alloys [5]. This one does not.
- **It is lead-free.** Lead is held under 1 %, with none added [1][6]. That matters on rails and strips that hands and feet touch.
- **It is hard.** Its Brinell hardness is 70–100: at least 70 as a sand casting, at least 80 as continuously cast bar, about 100 typical [1][6][15]. That suits walked inlays and strips.

Three other alloys were considered and set aside:
- **85-5-5-5 "ounce metal"** (C83600, LG2): the traditional gunmetal of art casting and hardware. It carries 5 % lead and is tan-gold rather than golden [5].
- **Silicon bronze** (C65500, C87300, "Everdur"): lead-free and widely used for sculpture, because it takes a wide range of patinas [5][7]. Its natural colour is "reddish old gold", redder than the museum's bronze [5].
- **"Architectural bronze"** (C38500): not a bronze at all, but a leaded brass of 57 % copper, 40 % zinc and 3 % lead [5].

The word "statuary bronze" in the trade names a finish, not an alloy: the brown-to-black surface, natural or chemical [5]. In the museum that finish is the working `patinated`.

## How it is made

Copper is melted and the tin added, and the metal is poured. For the sun clock's lines and letters, the founder casts by the lost-wax process: a wax model is invested in a refractory shell, the wax is burnt out, and bronze is poured in. The shell is then broken away and the sprues cut off. Finally the casting is chased: pits and the stubs of sprues are filed away and the detail re-cut, "until the telltale signs of the casting process are removed" [8]. Bar and hollow bar for the strips, rings and rails are cast continuously and machined.

It melts over 831–999 °C [6]. It weighs 8,800 kg per cubic metre [1]. It expands 18 millionths of its length for each degree Celsius, more than the stone around it, so long strips and rings need movement joints [1]. Its tensile strength is at least 250 MPa as a sand casting and 280 MPa as centrifugal or continuous casting [1][15]. Its Young's modulus is 102 GPa [15].

## How it is worked

Three workings apply to it, and gilding can be laid over it.

**Cast (`cast.bronze`).** This is the founder's finished casting before any colour. It is fettled and chased, then cleaned by a fine abrasive blast (CDA/NAAMM M42: 100–200 mesh at 207–621 kPa). That leaves an even, non-directional matte of bright metal, which is then waxed [5]. The node of the eye lattice is cast.

**Brushed (`brushed`).** This is the doorway strips, collar rings and car rings. The metal is polished in one direction with 180–240 grit belts at 30 m/s (M32 medium satin), which leaves fine parallel scratches along the piece [5]. The grain runs along the strip, parallel to the seam. Walked strips are left uncoated. Feet keep them bright, and the grain is renewed by hand rubbing along its direction.

**Patinated (`patinated`).** This is the sun clock's inlay. The founder colours the clean casting chemically by brushing 2–5 % sulfide solution onto the hot metal (C55 sulfide statuary; a laboratory version uses 3 % potassium sulfide) [5][9]. An oxide dip of copper salts at 85 °C to boiling may go first [5]. The surface becomes a thin film of cuprous oxide (cuprite) with copper sulfides, brown to near black [5]. The piece is waxed, and high points may be relieved to show the metal [5].

The film's phases are measured minerals [10]:
- cuprite has an index of 2.849 and reflects 23–32 % in its mirror direction, a cool grey, with its red seen only in light that has entered it;
- chalcocite reflects 29–38 %;
- black tenorite reflects 19–31 %.

**Gilt (`gilt`).** Gold leaf is laid over the bronze in places, for the gilt sun and the stops of the motto. See `gilt.md`.

## Its optics

Bronze is a metal, so its colour is its reflection. The measured colour of polished tin bronzes is known from a spectrophotometer with the specular reflection included [4]:

| Tin (wt%) | L* | a* | b* |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (copper) | 87.9 | 14.3 | 15.6 |
| 9.5 | 88.7 | 7.8 | 19.0 |
| 11 | 88.7 | 5.9 | 20.7 |

For 10 % tin this gives a normal-incidence reflectance (F0) of **[0.961, 0.693, 0.491]** in linear Rec.709. Correcting for the few per cent of diffuse light that the integrating sphere also collects gives about [0.868, 0.669, 0.488]. The true value lies between the two [4][11][12]. The correction was found by the same conversion on the paper's pure copper, set against copper's F0 from its measured optical constants [11].

No one has published the complex refractive index of a tin bronze. The edge tint at grazing angles, where metals whiten, is therefore recorded as unverified: a proxy from the nearest measured copper alloy.

## How it ages

**Indoors (`bronze.tarnish`).** Bare bronze darkens slowly: first a film of cuprite, then copper sulfides from traces of sulfur gases. It goes from golden to golden brown to brown. Indoors the change is "much slower and more subtle" than outdoors [5]. The film is self-limiting and protects the metal. Skin oils tarnish it faster [5]. Wax slows it. Indoors it never turns green.

**Under feet and hands (`bronze.wear`).** Where visitors walk or hold, grit scours the tarnish and the patina off the top faces down to bright metal. The directional grain is gradually replaced by a random fine polish. The CDA notes that patinas are "normally reserved for non-traffic areas" [5]. On the dial the museum shows the real result: bright, glossy metal where visitors stand and walk, brown in the recesses and along the edges against the stone. As roughness falls, gloss rises steeply [13].

**Outdoors (`bronze.weathering`).** In moist air the metal goes from salmon pink through deepening browns to the blue-green of a mature patina. That patina is mostly brochantite (basic copper sulfate) with basic copper carbonate; near the sea the shares of sulfates, chlorides and carbonates differ [5], and the chloride atacamite forms under saline conditions [10]. In arid air it stays brown [5]. The change is fast at first, then almost stops. Horizontal faces go first [5]. The CDA illustrates a copper roof after more than 20 years in humid air, and a mature patina "characteristic of over 80 years' exposure in a humid location" [5]. Tin bronze wetted with acid rain turns a dull brown (L* 49.9, a* 1.1, b* 9.4) [14]. A natural patina 9 years old in a city is 5–10 µm thick [9].

## What to look for in a photograph

- Bright tin bronze is a warm golden metal: less red than copper, less yellow than brass.
- A patinated piece is deep brown, near black in the hollows, with a waxy sheen and bright metal on the high points.
- A brushed strip shows fine parallel lines, and its highlight is drawn out across them.
- Walked and handled places are bright and glossy, and their edges and recesses stay brown.
- Green means weather; there is none indoors.

## Its evidence

The bronze is calibrated from **tier (a), measured tables**: the colour table above [4], copper's optical constants [11], and the minerals' reflectance tables [10]. Architecture says metals and glass are calibrated mainly from (a).

No CC0 photograph of real bronze was found on this PC. The two bronze sets of the old build are procedurally generated textures, not photographs, and are rejected as evidence:
- `windows/SourceArt/PBR/M1`, ambientCG Metal015, "patinated bronze";
- `windows/SourceArt/PBR/M2`, ambientCG Metal009, "brushed metal", tagged silver/steel.

Tier (b) evidence would settle what the tables cannot: the patina's diffuse colour and the brushed grain's anisotropy. The specimens are ColorChecker photographs of CuSn10 coupons, one polished, one brushed to M32 and one patinated to C55.
