# Roman travertine

Travertine is the museum's stone. It is the lapis Tiburtinus of the Romans: a banded, porous limestone that
thermal springs laid down as calcite in the plain below Tivoli, and that has been quarried there since Roman
times. In the museum it is sawn vein-cut, honed on walls, piers and plinths, and polished where a floor
should hold a soft reflection. It runs through every doorway as the threshold.

The record is `Data/Matter/Substances/travertine.json`. Its workings are `sawn.vein-cut`, `sawn.cross-cut`,
`honed`, `polished.stone` and `bush-hammered`; `flamed` is excluded. Its histories are `stone.foot-traffic-wear`,
`stone.pore-soiling` and `stone.dissolution-outdoors`. Each number below is in the record, next to its unit and
its source.

## How it forms

Water rises along a fault from depth near the Colli Albani volcano, 30 km east of Rome. It carries calcium
bicarbonate and a great deal of dissolved carbon dioxide. At the surface the water loses its carbon dioxide, and
calcite comes out of solution as thin crusts over the vents, the pools and the terraces below them. The crusts build
up layer on layer and trap gas bubbles as they grow. That is why travertine is porous, and why its pores are
arranged in layers.

At Tivoli the deposit covers about 20 km². It is 60 m thick on average and over 85 m on its western side, above the
fault where the springs and sinkholes still are. Uranium-series dating puts the start of deposition at about
115,000 years ago and its end at about 30,000 years ago [De Filippis 2011]. The deposit is a succession of
depositional benches separated by five main erosional surfaces [De Filippis 2011]. The benches are the stone's
bed, and the mason cuts relative to them.

Specimens from the Cava Morelli quarry at Tivoli are 85.6 to 99.9 % calcium carbonate. Their bulk density is 2250 to
2690 kg/m³ and their total porosity 0.3 to 16.7 % [Mancini 2019]. The trade's Travertino Romano Classico has an
apparent density of 2491 kg/m³ and absorbs 0.6 % of its mass in water [Alberti]. Its colour runs from milky white to
walnut, with yellow to red shades from the iron oxides taken up as it formed [Alberti; NSI].

The Natural Stone Institute's manual says Tivoli's springs were heated by Vesuvius. That is a slip. The geology
places the deposit by the Colli Albani [De Filippis 2011], and the record follows the geology.

## What light does in it

Travertine is calcite: a birefringent crystal with two refractive indices. The ordinary index is 1.658 and the
extraordinary 1.486 at 589 nm [Ghosh 1999; Handbook of Mineralogy]. A face cut through many small grains in random
orientations reflects about 5.3 % of the light straight back. A single grain can reflect between 3.8 and 6.1 %,
depending on its orientation. These values are derived in the record from Ghosh's dispersion formula, and the formula
is also tabulated from 400 to 700 nm. The polish does not change this reflectance. The finish only decides how sharp
the reflection is.

Below the surface, light scatters between the grains and the pores. No one has published subsurface measurements
of travertine that this research found. The nearest measured calcite stone is Jensen's white marble (2001), in which
diffuse light fades by a factor of e over about 8.5 mm in red and 4 mm in blue. Travertine's pores and iron oxides
will shorten that. The record keeps the marble's values only as a reference.

**Its colour, measured.** No colour measurement of Tivoli travertine was found. Two Spanish travertines were
measured, and they are the best evidence there is:

| Olivillo, filled | polished | 62.0 | 5.5 | 15.7 | 0.30 |

The Olivillo rows are from Urosevic et al. (2010), and the Travertino Amarillo rows from Molina et al. (2020).
The luminous reflectance is derived from L\*.

The measurements teach two things:
- **Polishing darkens and warms the stone.** It removes the rough first surface that scattered white light into the
  reading, so the body colour shows undiluted. The Olivillo measurement used a Minolta CR-210 viewing at 0°, an
  instrument that lights the sample diffusely, so it hardly counts the mirror reflection.
- **The old design colours are lighter than every measurement.** The old build's travertine colours (#E6DDCF,
  #E8E0D2, #E1D6C4) have L\* 86 to 89. They were chosen by eye against the renderings, and the record lists them
  under *unverified*. Matter.Calibrated will judge them against a real specimen.

**Wet stone.** A wet face darkens. Water in the pores lowers the refractive-index step at each grain, and the film
traps diffusely reflected light by total internal reflection, so more of it is absorbed [Lekner & Dorf 1988].

## How it is worked

**The cut.** A block can be sawn two ways, and the two faces look like different stones [NSI]:
- **Vein-cut** is sawn across the bed. The face shows the layers' edges as long, wavy, parallel bands, and the voids
  are drawn out along them. This is the museum's cut.
- **Cross-cut**, or *fleuri*, is sawn along the bed. The face lies within one layer, so it shows soft clouds with
  larger, rounder, scattered voids. Alberti notes that the Colosseum's travertine is cross-cut.

The two cuts differ in strength, so the cut a project uses must be tested [NSI]. A cross-cut floor can open holes
under heavy traffic, where thin-walled voids lie just below the face [NSI]. In the records, the cut is the working
`sawn.vein-cut` or `sawn.cross-cut`. Its angle to the bed is 90° or 0°, and Form's per-piece bed frame carries it.

Slabs come off a gang saw (with steel shot, chat or sand in a slurry, or with diamond blades), a diamond wire or a
disc. A diamond-gang-sawn face is fairly smooth, with parallel markings [NSI]. Standard thicknesses are 20, 30, 50 and
60 mm [NSI].

**Filling.** Travertine for floors and other horizontal surfaces is usually filled before it is finished. The filler
is portland cement, either natural grey or tinted to match, or clear or coloured epoxy or polyester resin [NSI].
Holes larger than 2 cm are plugged with a chip of travertine cemented below the surface [NSI]. One Spanish maker fills
the large pores with a beige gypsum plaster, then dresses the face with epoxy-silicone resin before polishing. The
resin closes the near-surface pores: open porosity fell from 8.2 to 7.3 % [Urosevic 2010]. Travertine is also sold
open-pore, honed or brushed [Travertino Romano Group].

**Honing** grinds the face flat with progressively finer abrasives and stops before it shines [Molina 2020]. In trade
practice it ends at 400 or 800 grit [trade guide]. The NSI describes honed travertine as a satin-smooth surface with
little or no gloss, and recommends honed floors for commercial use [NSI]. A marble face ground with 120-grade abrasive
reads under 5 gloss units at any angle [3M patent]. As the grit gets finer, the roughness falls long before the gloss
rises: the gloss climbs steeply only after about 320 grit. Gloss follows roughness less closely in travertine than in
micritic limestone [Yavuz 2011].

**Polishing** carries on to 1500 and 3000 grit and finishes with a polishing powder or an oxalic-acid compound [trade
guide]. It brings high gloss, about 75 gloss units or more at 60° on marble [3M patent]. Travertine is filled before it
is polished.

**Bush-hammering** strikes the face with a hammer that has steel teeth. On a Spanish travertine it left an areal
roughness Sa of 0.38 mm, against 0.24 mm sawn and 0.22 mm honed. The paper names no filter, so these
videomicroscope values presumably include the pores [Molina 2020].

**Flaming** is not for travertine. The quarrier Polycor writes that limestone and marble cannot be flamed, because
calcium carbonate does not spall the way the quartz and feldspar of granite do. Another supplier says some hard
limestones can take a flame [Connecticut Stone]. The record follows the quarrier's rule.

## How it ages

**Feet wear it** (`stone.foot-traffic-wear`). Shoes carry quartz grit, of Mohs hardness 7, onto calcite, of hardness
3. Every step drags harder grains over the stone [Handbook of Mineralogy]:
1. The polish goes first, in lanes along the walking lines and in a fan at each doorway.
2. Then the stone itself wears, following Archard's law (volume ∝ load × distance ÷ hardness).
3. Treads and thresholds dish, and their arrises round.

Travertino Romano's wide-wheel abrasion groove is 21.3 mm [Alberti]. The NSI asks for an abrasion hardness of at least
10 on floors and 12 under heavy traffic. It warns that stones differing by more than 5 in one floor wear unevenly
[NSI]. That rule matters wherever travertine meets another stone in a floor. No wear rate per footfall was found.

**Dirt settles in its pores** (`stone.pore-soiling`). In historic interiors most dust comes from visitors. Deposition
is highest beside the routes and halves with every metre away from them. It is heavy at floor level and again between
0.8 and 1.5 m, and it cements in place above about 65 % relative humidity [Lithgow et al. 2005]. Open voids collect
this dirt; filled ones cannot. On honed open-pore travertine the voids along the walking lines turn into dark dots and
dashes, and grey cement filler shows as flat matt patches.

**Rain dissolves it** (`stone.dissolution-outdoors`). Clean rain alone removes about 18.8 µm of carbonate stone for
every metre of rain [Lipfert 1989, via HYPERION D4.5]. Acid rain and dry-deposited SO₂ and HNO₃ add to that. In an
accelerated test, fine-grained micrite receded about twice as fast as coarse spar, and clay-rich stones receded
fastest [HYPERION D4.5]. So an exterior travertine face loses its polish, its fine layers sink between the sparry
ones, and its voids open wider. De-icing salts damage most travertine [NSI].

## What to look for in a photograph

- **Vein-cut:** parallel, wavy bands of ivory, cream and honey, with voids drawn out along them like small lenses.
- **Cross-cut:** clouds instead of bands, and larger, rounder voids scattered over the face.
- **Voids:** open ones read as dark dots and dashes. Filled ones are flush, grey where cement was used and glossier
  where resin was.
- **Finish:** honed stone gives broad, soft reflections with no sharp image. Polished stone gives sharp reflections
  and a darker, warmer colour.
- **Joints:** neighbouring slabs are different slices of one bed. The tone carries across a joint; the lines do not.
- **Wear:** on floors, the gloss goes first along the walking lines and at doorways.

## Evidence

No photograph of real travertine is on this PC. The old build's travertine set (S1, ambientCG Travertine009) is
procedural, as its own asset page says. Its derived maps, the recoloured `T_travertine_veincut.png`, and the iOS app's

Travertine stands only on measured tables (tier a), and its colour comes from Spanish travertines. A tier (b)
specimen would close the gap: ColorChecker photographs of Travertino Romano Classico under one known light, in these
states:
- vein-cut and cross-cut;
- honed open;
- honed filled;
- polished.

## Not verified

These values are listed under `unverified` in the record and are not data:
- a colour measurement of Tivoli travertine itself;
- the old design colours;
- the old build's void fraction (4 %) and void depth (2.5 mm), both read from a procedural image;
- band spacing;
- block and bench sizes;
- the capillary water-absorption coefficient that the surface law needs;
- slip resistance;
- the absorption of sound by open pores;
- travertine's own subsurface scattering;
- a compressive value of 76.1 MPa that was seen only in a search summary.
