# Cream crackle marble

The Rotunda is built of a cream marble crossed by fine grey veins that join into a crackle. The design says so, and
the rendering shows it: pale cream ashlar with faint grey veining on the walls and the sixteen fluted pilasters. The
same marble forms the sun clock's rising drum, and it continues, glass-smooth, into the future rooms. This dossier
identifies the real stone behind that description and records what it is.

The record is `Data/Matter/Substances/cream-marble.json`. Its workings are `sawn.vein-cut`, `sawn.cross-cut`,
`honed`, `polished.stone` and `bush-hammered`; `flamed` is excluded. Its histories are `stone.foot-traffic-wear`,
`stone.pore-soiling` and `stone.dissolution-outdoors`. Each number below is in the record, next to its unit and its
source.

## Which stone it is

"Cream marble with grey crackle veins" names a look, not a quarry. The old build drew the look as fine grey veins
around cells 25 to 40 cm across, with a finer set in patches, over a soft cloud. Its texture came from an image
generator and a procedural material, not from a stone. So the question is which real stone has a cream ground cut by
a *network* of fine dark veins.

**The answer is Crema Marfil, from Monte El Coto at Pinoso, Alicante.**
- **It is cream and compact.** The maker describes it as having "refined tones and discrete natural veins"
  [Levantina].
- **Its veins form a network.** A study of its breakage found five families of discontinuity planes crossing the
  stone [López-Buendía et al. 2013]:
  - open stylolites;
  - partly open stylolites;
  - stylolites cemented with iron oxides and clay minerals, the dark grey-brown seams;
  - stylolites cemented with calcite;
  - white calcite veins.

  Seams at many angles make cells, and cells are what the design calls a crackle.
- **It is a commercial marble.** It meets the Marble Institute of America's requirements [Ordóñez et al. 1996].
- **It is quarried at scale:** about 500,000 m³ a year from some twenty quarries on one mountain [Litos]. Its maker
  calls its colour "the most appreciated in the world" [Levantina].

**Botticino Classico**, from east of Brescia, is the classical Italian cream "marble" and was the other candidate. It
is a compact Jurassic limestone, partly dolomitised, white to ivory with hints of chestnut [Cooperativa Cavatori
Botticino; HYPERION D4.5]. Its stylolites are thin, chestnut-coloured, and run almost parallel to the bedding
[Cavatori]. Its veining therefore reads as lines, not as cells, and it was set aside. Its data are kept in the record
under `alternativesConsidered` in case the owner prefers it.

Neither stone is a metamorphic marble. Both are limestones that take a polish, sold as marble in the trade. That
changes nothing about their optics. Both are calcite, and calcite's optics are the same in a limestone as in a
statuary marble.

## How it forms

About 50 million years ago, in the early Eocene, the shells of foraminifera (nummulites) and lime mud gathered on the
sea floor [Levantina]. They were cemented into a biosparite: shell grains in clear crystalline calcite.
Later the rock was cut by stylolites and by fractures [López-Buendía et al. 2013]:
- the stylolites are jagged seams, some open and some cemented with iron oxides and clay minerals or with calcite;
- the fractures were healed with white calcite.

Together the stylolites and veins are the crackle. The trade grades the stone by tone and by the density of its
veins, and names varieties with fossil grains, a wavy "water" figure, pinkish or greenish tones, white coin-sized
"flowers", and white limestone veins [Litos].

The stylolites are also where the stone is weak. They are planes of weakness that restrict its processing, so thin
slabs are impregnated with epoxy resin under vacuum [López-Buendía et al. 2013]. A breakage rate for untreated
2 cm slabs (10 to 15 %, with more than 95 % of breaks along stylolites) was attributed to the same paper, but its
abstract does not contain it, so the record lists it under *unverified*.

## Its measured properties

The maker's tests of Crema Marfil Coto [Levantina]:

| Property | Value | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Apparent density | 2690 kg/m³ | UNE-EN 1936 |
| Open porosity | 0.5 % | UNE-EN 1936 |
| Water absorption | 0.2 % (0.34 % by ASTM C97) | UNE-EN 13755 |
| Flexural breaking stress | 9.7 MPa | UNE-EN 12372 |
| Compressive breaking stress | 157 MPa | ASTM C170 |
| Wide-wheel abrasion groove | 19.5 mm | UNE-EN 14157 |
| Loss after 56 freeze-thaw cycles | 15 % of flexural breaking stress | UNE-EN 12371 |
| Light reflectance value | 64.4 % | not stated |

The light reflectance value corresponds to a CIE lightness L\* of about 84. The maker's sheet also prints an ASTM
C241 abrasion hardness of 4.1. That is out of line with the stone's EN 14157 groove, which is close to travertine's,
so it is kept under *unverified* until it is confirmed.

## What light does in it

Crema Marfil is calcite, so its refractive indices and its normal-incidence reflectance are travertine's:
- the ordinary index is 1.658 and the extraordinary 1.486 at 589 nm [Ghosh 1999];
- a polished face reflects about 5.3 % straight back.

With only 0.5 % open porosity it is far denser than travertine. It has few open voids, and its pattern lies in the
body of the stone, not in holes.

Under the surface, light diffuses between the grains. Jensen's white marble (2001) is the nearest measured calcite
stone: its diffuse light fades by a factor of e over about 8.5 mm in red and 4 mm in blue. Crema Marfil is
fine-grained and carries iron-oxide seams, so it will be less translucent. It was not measured, and the record keeps
the marble's numbers only as a reference.

The veins differ from the ground in what fills them. The stylolites are lined with iron oxides and clay minerals,
which colour them against the cream [López-Buendía et al. 2013], and the calcite veins are white [Litos]. The crackle
is these seams seen together.

Polishing does to it what it does to travertine: the colour deepens and the veins sharpen. A wet face darkens, for
the same reason as travertine [Lekner & Dorf 1988].

## How it is worked

Crema Marfil is sold polished, honed, aged and bush-hammered, in slabs 10, 12, 20 and 30 mm thick [Levantina]:
- **Honed** faces have a low, even sheen, and the veins stay soft.
- **Polished** faces take a high gloss: about 75 gloss units or more at 60° on marble [3M patent]. Polishing is carried
  through 1500 and 3000 grit to a polishing powder or an oxalic-acid compound [trade guide].
- **Bush-hammered** faces are rough enough to grip in the wet, reading above 45 on the pendulum [Levantina].
- **Flaming** does not apply: it is a finish for granite, not for calcite stones [Polycor].

The maker's pendulum slip values, for the finishes it lists:

| Finish | Wet | Dry |
|---|---|---|
| Aged | 20 | above 45 |
| Bush-hammered | above 45 | above 45 |
| Rough | 55 | above 45 |
| Sandblasted | 57 | 71 |

Honed and polished values are not on the sheet.

The stone is bedded like any sediment, but its veins fall into several families. How its figure changes between a
cut across and a cut along the bed was not found.

## How it ages

**Feet wear it** (`stone.foot-traffic-wear`). This matters where it is a floor: in the future rooms, and on the dial
if the Rotunda lays the dial in it. Quartz grit under shoes scratches calcite, so the gloss goes first along the
walking lines [Handbook of Mineralogy; Yavuz 2011]. Its abrasion groove (19.5 mm) is close to travertine's (21.3 mm),
which suggests the two will wear at similar rates where they meet in a floor. The NSI's rule that stones in one floor
stay within 5 of each other in abrasion hardness is stated for ASTM C241 values, and those are not yet confirmed for
this stone.

**Dirt** (`stone.pore-soiling`) finds little to hold in so dense a stone. It gathers in the joints, the scratches and
the flutes of the pilasters, heaviest near the floor and beside the walking routes [Lithgow et al. 2005].

**Rain** (`stone.dissolution-outdoors`) is not a concern for the Rotunda, which is enclosed. Outdoors the grain-size
rule would apply [HYPERION D4.5]:
- the fine matrix recedes faster than the coarse calcite of the veins, so the white veins would stand slightly proud;
- clay-rich stylolites recede fastest, so the dark ones would sink;
- the crackle would become a faint relief.

## What to look for in a photograph

- A cream ground, even in tone, crossed in several directions by fine, jagged seams that join into irregular cells.
- Seams of two kinds: grey-brown toothed stylolites, and paler, smoother calcite veins.
- In some slabs, small fossil grains, white spots or wavy figures; grades differ in how dense their veins are.
- A finish that shows in the veins: honed stone has a low, even sheen and soft veins, while polished stone has a deep
  gloss in which the veins sharpen and the ground warms.
- In thin pieces, cracks along the seams, sometimes mended with resin.

## Evidence

One photograph on this PC shows the kind of stone: Poly Haven's *marble_01* by Rob Tuytel (CC0), in the old build's
library as S3. The provider describes it as "smooth, even cream-beige tiles with subtle veining and low sheen". It
shows a wall of honed cream compact limestone with a network of fine dark veins, small pores, thin joints and blocks
of differing tone.

Its source does not name the stone, and its exposure is uncalibrated. It is therefore tier (c) evidence for the
pattern only: the vein network, and the tone varying from block to block. The record gives its mean colour, computed
here, with that warning.

## Not verified

These values are listed under `unverified` in the record and are not data:
- Crema Marfil's a\* and b\*;
- the crackle's cell size of 25 to 40 cm, which is the old design value;
- the printed ASTM abrasion hardness;
- the thin-slab breakage rate (10 to 15 %) and the share of breaks along stylolites (over 95 %), which are not in
  the López-Buendía abstract;
- the honed and polished slip values;
- the capillary coefficient;
- its own subsurface scattering;
- the geology of stylolite formation, for which no source was read;
- Botticino's use in the Vittoriano, which was seen only in a search summary;
- how the figure changes with the cut.
